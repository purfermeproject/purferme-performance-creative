# Millets & Oats Chocolate Cookies

**Campaign idea:** *Feels like a treat. Made differently.*
**Role in the portfolio:** The desire product. The best trial driver for families with kids, and the best proof that the brand isn't "health food".
**Default route:** A (craving-led). Lead with the chocolate, and let the formulation arrive after the craving.

> ### ⚠️ Critical: most facts for this product need verification
> The brief gives the core tension and positioning, but **not the full ingredient list**. Before any ad goes live, confirm:
> - Full ingredient list: which millets? oats? cocoa or chocolate chips? what kind of chocolate?
> - Sweetener: jaggery, cane sugar, or something else? (**Do not assume jaggery.**)
> - Fat: cow butter? (**Do not assume.**)
> - Which "no" claims apply: no maida? no palm oil? no refined sugar? no preservatives?
> - Nutrition panel, pack sizes, price, allergens
>
> Until then, ads can use the **craving + "millets and oats inside" + "read the back"** structure only, with the real back of the pack shown on screen.

---

### A. Primary audience
Parents of children aged roughly 4–12, who buy chocolate cookies for kids' snacks and tiffin but increasingly check labels.

### B. Secondary audience
- Adult chocolate lovers (20–35) who self-treat, especially office snackers.
- Gifting and hosting (guests, festive boxes).
- Existing Breakfast Cookie buyers looking for a "treat" variant (a cross-sell).

### C. Main consumer problem
Kids (and adults) want chocolate cookies. Parents want to know what's in them. Right now, that means either (a) saying no, (b) saying yes and not reading the label, or (c) buying a "healthy" version the kids reject.

### D. Main desire
*"I want to say yes to the chocolate cookie, and actually mean it."*

### E. Current alternative
- Mainstream chocolate/cream biscuits and cookies
- "Healthier" cookies that don't taste like a treat
- Rationing: "only on weekends"
- Home-baked, occasionally

### F. Why that alternative may disappoint
- Mainstream: the parent feels a small internal debate every time.
- "Healthy" versions: the kid notices, and the pack sits unfinished.
- Rationing: turns snacks into a negotiation.

### G. Product mechanism
**The treat is kept; the base is rethought.** The chocolate cookie experience is built on millets and oats instead of the usual base *(verify the specifics, and swap in the verified "instead of" list once confirmed)*.

### H. Strongest proof
1. **A kid's real first-bite reaction.** The strongest possible taste proof for this product.
2. **The blind taste test**: "I told them these were regular chocolate cookies."
3. **The back of the pack.**

### I. Emotional payoff
Being the **yes-parent**, and a **shared treat** with no internal debate.

### J. Best 10 hooks

### M. Best 3 static ad concepts
1. **The break.** A giant macro of the cookie breaking, dark background, rich lighting. Headline: "Feels like a treat." Sub: "Made differently. Millets & oats inside." The pack in the bottom right.
2. **"Wait… you said YES?"** A kid's delighted, shocked face. Pack in hand. Sub: "Chocolate cookies you've already read the back of."
3. **Nani ✅ Mummy ✅.** A two-generation approval meme with the pack in the centre.

### N. Best CTA styles
- Permission: "Say yes to this one."
- Treat: "Treat yourself. Read the back later."
- Kid test: "Try it on your toughest critic."
- Trial: "Start with one pack."

### O. Claims to avoid
- ❌ "Healthy chocolate cookie", "guilt-free", "sin-free", "zero guilt"
- ❌ "Good for kids' growth / brain / energy / immunity"
- ❌ "Sugar-free" or "no sugar" (unless formulation verified *and* legally compliant)
- ❌ Any "no ___" claim not yet verified for this specific product
- ❌ "Better than [brand]" or "tastes exactly like [brand]"
- ❌ "Kids will love it" as a guarantee. Show, don't promise.
- ❌ Any implication it can be eaten without limit
